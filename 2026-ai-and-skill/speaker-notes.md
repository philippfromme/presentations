## 01 — Cognitive Debt

*The accumulating loss of understanding you take on when AI produces work you never actually grasp.*

- An **EEG study** ("Your Brain on ChatGPT," MIT Media Lab). They wired people up while
  writing essays. Three groups: brain-only, Google, ChatGPT.
- The LLM group's brains lit up the least — weakest connectivity of the three. They felt
  the least ownership of their work, and **couldn't quote a sentence from the essay they'd
  written minutes before.** It went through them, not into them.
- Storey's team-level reframe: software health needs three layers aligned — **intent**
  (what it's for), **code** (how it's built), **shared understanding** (how the team
  reasons about change). AI produces code faster than the other two keep up, so they drift.
- The irony: AI **pays down technical debt** (cleaner code, more tests) while **running up
  cognitive debt** — a beautiful codebase nobody understands.
- Also **intent debt**: when the *why* never gets written down, humans and agents optimize
  for the wrong thing. Tests pass, product drifts.

## 02 — Cognitive Surrender

*Handing your judgment to the AI and accepting its answer without checking whether it's right.*

- Extends Kahneman: System 1 = gut/fast, System 2 = slow/deliberate, **System 3 = the AI
  outside your head.** System 3 doesn't just assist — it can **suppress** the other two.
- **Surrender ≠ offloading.** Offloading is deliberate and healthy (calculator, linter).
  Surrender is giving up the judgment of whether the answer is even right. The tell: did
  you *choose* to defer, or just stop thinking?
- The study (N = 1,372): over half the AI's answers were wrong, and when people consulted
  it and it was wrong, they followed it anyway **~4 of 5 times.** Accuracy swung ~+25 pts
  when AI was right, ~−15 pts when wrong (below the no-AI baseline).
- AI access **raised confidence ~12 pts** despite half the answers being wrong. More sure,
  less correct — why the debt stays invisible; it *feels* like competence.
- Helped but didn't cure: time pressure makes it worse; paying for accuracy + instant
  feedback more than doubled how often people overrode bad AI.

## 03 — Skill Formation

*How AI changes whether you actually build expertise while learning something new.*

- Randomized experiment: developers learn a library none knew (Trio, async Python). Half
  get AI, half don't. Then quiz everyone **with the AI removed.**
- AI group scored ~17% lower (≈2 letter grades) and **weren't even faster.** Pay in
  understanding, don't buy speed.
- The skills that eroded most were **code reading and debugging** — exactly what you need
  to *supervise* AI. So AI erodes the ability to catch AI's mistakes.
- The no-AI group hit ~3x more errors — and fighting through errors is **where the skill
  formed.** AI smooths the road; the learning was in the bumps.
- Quote: AI users felt "lazy" with "gaps in understanding." Only ~20% were faster.
  "Productivity is not a shortcut to competence."

## 04 — Skill Atrophy

*The skills you already have decaying because AI does the reps you used to do yourself.*

- Survey (319 knowledge workers): higher confidence **in the AI** → *less* critical
  thinking; higher confidence **in yourself** → *more*. Trust the tool, stop checking it.
- Bainbridge's *Ironies of Automation* (1983 — predates all this): automate a task, the
  human stops practicing, skill decays — yet the human is kept on to take over the rare
  hard case the automation can't. Least equipped exactly when most needed. Autopilot &
  pilots; now you & the agent.
- "Automation complacency": with a reliable tool people stop monitoring. Does **not** go
  away with experience, hard to train away.
- The **exoskeleton**: lifts more today, but your muscles aren't working — take it off and
  you're weaker. Writing/technical boosts **vanished** the moment people worked unaided.
- **Novices get the biggest boost from AI** — and they're the ones meant to be *building*
  skill. The group that looks most helped has the most to lose.

## 05 — Brain Fry

*The acute mental exhaustion that comes from supervising AI, not from doing the work.*

- Symptom: a "fog" or "buzzing," can't focus, slower decisions, headaches — a mental
  hangover. 14% of AI-using workers report it by name. Study of 1,488 U.S. workers.
- Core finding: the **oversight** fries you, not the work. Watching and double-checking an
  agent costs *more* mental effort than doing the task yourself. Monitoring is high-load,
  no flow state — you stay alert, can't trust, can't switch off.
- Tipping point ~**three simultaneous tools**: productivity climbs to two, flattens at
  three, then drops. More agents ≠ more done.
- Costs: among the fried, **major errors +39%**, intent to quit jumped 25% → 34% — often
  your *best* people (heaviest users) burn out first.
- The lever: shaped by **how**, not how much. Using AI to kill boring toil *lowered*
  burnout ~15%. Managers who answer AI questions → 15% less fatigue; "figure it out
  yourself" → 5% more ("AI orphan tax"). Rewarding token/line-count metrics makes it worse.
