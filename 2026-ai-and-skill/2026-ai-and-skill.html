01
Cognitive debt
The accumulating loss of understanding you take on when AI produces work you never actually grasp.
The hidden cost of letting the model do the thinking.
Coined by MIT Media Lab's EEG study "Your Brain on ChatGPT." The LLM group showed the weakest brain connectivity of the three groups, the lowest sense of ownership of their work, and couldn't quote essays they'd written minutes earlier.
— Kosmyna et al., arXiv:2506.08872
At team level: nobody can explain how the system works.
GenAI can pay down technical debt while running up cognitive debt. Invisible until changes start failing — surprising side effects, slow onboarding, low bus factor.
— Storey, ACM Queue

02
Cognitive surrender
Handing your judgment to the AI and accepting its answer without checking whether it's right.
System 3: thinking, outsourced.
Shaw & Nave extend Kahneman — System 1 (intuition), System 2 (deliberation), System 3 (the AI). Surrender = taking System 3's answer with little scrutiny, muting both your own systems. Not offloading a task — abdicating the judgment of whether it's even right.
— Shaw & Nave, Tri-System Theory (SSRN)
People follow the AI even when it's wrong.
3 experiments, N = 1,372. When the AI was wrong, people followed it anyway on ~4 of 5 trials — accuracy fell below the no-AI baseline. Worse: having the AI raised their confidence, even when its answers were wrong.
— Shaw & Nave (SSRN)

03
Skill formation
How AI changes whether you actually build expertise while learning something new.
Learning with AI: not faster, and worse.
Developers learned a new async library (Trio) with or without AI. The AI group scored ~17% lower on comprehension — and weren't significantly faster. Biggest gaps: code reading and debugging, the very skills you need to supervise AI.
— Shen & Tamkin, arXiv:2601.20245
How you use AI matters more than whether you use it.
Low scorers delegated. High scorers stayed engaged — asked why, generated-then-read. It's effort of mind, not fingers: typing the code by hand vs pasting it made no difference.
— Shen & Tamkin (arXiv)

04
Skill atrophy
The skills you already have decaying because AI does the reps you used to do yourself.
Use it or lose it — and AI makes it easy not to use it.
319 knowledge workers: higher trust in AI predicted less critical thinking. Bainbridge's Ironies of Automation — automate the task and the human's skill decays, yet they're kept on to take over exactly the hard cases the automation can't handle.
— Lee et al., CHI 2025 · Bainbridge, 1983
AI is an exoskeleton — the gains vanish when it's removed.
Performance boosts on writing and technical tasks disappeared when workers later worked unaided: the underlying skill was never built. And novices — who get the biggest boost — have the most to lose.
— Shen & Tamkin (arXiv)

05
Brain fry
The acute mental exhaustion that comes from supervising AI, not from doing the work.
Overseeing AI fries your brain.
Watching and double-checking an agent costs more mental effort than doing the task yourself — +14% effort, +19% information overload. Symptoms: a "fog," lost focus, slower decisions, headaches. 14% of AI users report it.
— Bedard et al., HBR/BCG
More tools isn't better past a point.
Productivity dips after ~3 simultaneous tools. Among those frying: +39% major errors, intent to quit jumped 25% → 34% — often the heaviest users are top talent. But replacing toil lowered burnout ~15%, and supportive managers cut fatigue 15%.
— Bedard et al. (HBR)
